Campus demand — where student housing actually pencils

The Locator X Investor Guide · AGI Corp · AGI Future Foundation

The Locator X Investor Guide

Locator X is built on one conviction, verified here against 5,000 real Bay Area records: a property is an investment only if it pays you. This guide is the operating manual for that conviction — the doctrine, the method, the numbers, the laws, and the ninety-day plan — with every claim wired to the live data in the tabs above.

1. The Locator X doctrine

Every property in this app is judged by one test before any other: after real expenses and the mortgage, does money flow toward the owner or away? An asset puts money in your pocket every month. A liability takes it out, however handsome the address. Appreciation is real in the Bay Area — but Locator X treats it as the reward for owning a survivable building, never as the reason to buy a bleeding one.

That is why the map pins are colored by cash flow, why the dashboard's first tile counts assets, and why the score's heaviest weight is the asset test. The doctrine is not pessimism; it is sequencing. Cash flow first, appreciation second, story last.

2. The Method — five verbs, five tabs

Locator X turns the doctrine into a repeatable pipeline. Each verb is a tab; run them in order and a raw market becomes a ranked set of decisions.

VerbWhereWhat happens

One insight governs the journey: employees and the self-employed trade time for money; investors own systems that produce it. A salary is not the obstacle — it is the borrowing power that funds the first building. The Method is how a W-2 earner crosses to the asset column without gambling the crossing.

3. The Bay Area problem — and the six honest answers

The Bay Area's prices outran its rents decades ago. The 1% rule is a museum piece here; most ZIPs gross 3–6% before a single expense. Pretending otherwise is how buyers end up calling negative cash flow "forced savings." Locator X answers the problem instead of denying it.

4. Read the numbers like a lender

Five figures decide every deal in this app. The drawer computes them live; the Academy drills them until you can do it on a napkin at an open house.

MetricFormulaWhat it answers
NOIRent − vacancy − operating costsWhat the building earns before any loan exists.
Cap rateNOI ÷ priceThe building's own return, loan-blind — compare buildings with it.
Cash flowNOI − debt serviceThe asset test. Positive or it is not an asset.
Cash-on-cashAnnual cash flow ÷ cash inWhat your actual dollars earn.
DSCRNOI ÷ annual debt serviceThe lender's first question. 1.20+ is financeable; under 1.0 you feed the building.

Rents in Locator X default to the ZIP's rent-to-value ratio applied to the price — deliberately conservative. Before an offer, replace the estimate with three comps from a property manager; the sheet has a field waiting for them.

5. Leverage — other people's money, sized to survive

Debt is the multiplier that makes a thin Bay Area yield interesting or fatal. Locator X models four structures on every sheet, because the same building can be all four deals:

StructureTerms modeledBest for
FHA owner-occupied, 2–4 units3.5% down, MIP 0.55%/yr, self-sufficiency test on 3–4 unitsThe first building — house hacking
Conventional investor25% down at the market rateClean rentals with real coverage
DSCR loan25% down, rate ~0.85 above, qualifies on the propertySelf-employed and scaling buyers
Seller carry15% down at a negotiated 4.5%Long-held sellers with Prop 13 bases and patience

The Locator X rule for every structure: the rent pays the loan with room to spare, at today's rate plus one. If it only works at yesterday's rate, it doesn't work.

6. The silent partner — California's tax architecture

Proposition 13 freezes your assessed value at the purchase price and caps its growth at 2% a year — your tax stands still while rents move. It is also why every price in this app is trustworthy: a post-sale assessed value IS the price the buyer paid. Depreciation shelters cash flow on paper; the assessor's land/improvement split in every drawer is your starting allocation. A 1031 exchange rolls a Bay Area gain into the next building tax-deferred — the velocity-of-money move with the tax code's blessing. And rent regulation behaves like a tax: SF (pre-June-1979 multi-unit), Oakland (pre-1983), Berkeley (pre-1980) and the statewide AB 1482 cap all print on the research report before you offer.

7. Sourcing — the deals that never hit a portal

The same public records that power this map reveal the sellers who haven't decided yet. Long holds with 1980s tax bases. Mailing addresses that left the state decades ago. The ⚠ below-market transfers — partial-interest and family transfers that mark quiet ownership changes, priced by the comps agent at what they'd really trade for. Odd transfers plus a prepared, respectful letter is how off-market inventory is built; the Research tab's instant lookups open the recorder trail behind any parcel in two clicks. Ethics first, always: serve the person, then the spread.

8. The bench — six people before one offer

An investor-friendly agent who owns units. A broker fluent in FHA multi-unit and DSCR paper. A property manager whose rent comps you get before you buy. An attorney who knows the local rent ordinance. A CPA who has actually filed a cost segregation. A contractor who has permitted an ADU in your target city. Pay them well, and ask each the same question: what would you not buy?

9. The ninety-day runway

Days 1–30 — calibrate. Pick two ZIPs from the Market tab with yields above the Bay median. Set your buy box on the Underwrite tab. Run the Academy's Deal Analyst chair until the transfer check clears — the numbers should live in your head before they live in an offer. Get pre-approved for both an owner-occupied and an investor loan.

Days 31–60 — build flow. Auto-screen your matches weekly. Get manager rent comps for every survivor. Letter the long-hold owners in your two ZIPs. Tour everything that passes the buy box; underwrite everything you tour, in the sheet, same day.

Days 61–90 — close or walk clean. Inspect for the Bay Area big four: foundation, soft-story, knob-and-tube, sewer lateral. Confirm the rent regime and the real leases. Offer the solver's number with 3% earnest money and short honest contingencies. If the DSCR at closing dips under 1.0 with verified rents — walk. The pipeline exists so that no single deal is ever the only deal.

US growth corridors · where the next demand is being announced

Five metros where the jobs are arriving faster than the housing

Outside this app’s current footprint, a small number of US metros have very large announced industrial projects — semiconductor fabs, EV and battery plants, hyperscale data centres — that imply real demand for rental housing. This tab ranks five of them on one ratio: announced direct jobs against housing units actually permitted in the last twelve months. Both halves are published figures and both carry their source.

An announced job is not a filled job. Intel’s Ohio fabs have slipped at least five years; Micron’s first Clay fab is a 2030 story. Announcements get downsized, delayed and cancelled, and every row here says where it stands. A permit is not a delivered unit either, and household formation per job is not one. Use the ratio to rank these five against each other on the same basis — never as a count of households.

Capital and jobs are deliberately never combined into a single score. A data centre and an assembly plant with the same price tag employ wildly different numbers of people, which is exactly what the capital-per-job chart is there to show you.

The corridor field · every growth area on two measures at once

Where the jobs are announced, and where you could actually buy

One bubble per metro, positioned on any two of nine corridor measures, sized by how much stock this catalogue holds there, and subdivided inside by what that stock actually is — a recorded multifamily use, some other recorded use, a zoning permission, or a parcel whose source publishes no use class at all. The disagreements are the point: a corridor can rank first on announced jobs per housing unit permitted and last on anything you could underwrite.

A metro missing one of the two measures is drawn as a small dashed ring rather than placed at zero, because a series a government does not publish and a value of zero are different facts. Every measure is computed from figures already carried on the corridor record with their own source and as-of date, or counted from the records in this edition. Nothing is modelled.

Predictions · every forecast carries the error it earned in backtest

What the record says is about to happen — and how often that has been wrong

A forecast without a measured error is a guess in a suit. Every projection on this page is produced the same way: the last six months are held out, a model is fitted on the twelve before them, and the model is scored against the months it never saw. The band you see is that measured backtest error, not an assumed normal distribution around a trend line. Where the backtest was poor, the page says so and widens the band rather than hiding it.

This engine forecasts geographies — ZIPs, sectors, corridors — and refuses to forecast an individual parcel. A single property has no time series; what it has is an index applied to it. Anything claiming to predict one house’s price to the dollar is projecting the neighbourhood and putting a street address on the answer.

The investment standard · written down so it can be argued with

What this platform means by a good investment

Every screen here has implied a definition without stating one. This is the definition: a named list of requirements in five categories, each a function of the published record, each returning a status you can check. Every property in the app is tested against all of them, and the result appears on the property itself.

A requirement can come back meets, fails, or not published — and the third is the one that matters. A county that publishes no year built has not made a building old, and it has not made it new. Folding that into “fails” rejects properties for a gap in a county schema; folding it into “meets” is worse. Here it stays visible, and the count of unanswerable requirements travels with every score.

Packages · how this platform grows

Twelve pages, one application, a hard ceiling of 16 MB

A published page cannot exceed 16 MB. The application itself — map, underwriting engine, forecast field, pattern miner, standard — costs about 2.6 MB of that and is identical in every edition, which leaves roughly 12.4 MB for parcels: around 300,000 properties. So this platform does not grow by making one page bigger. It grows by adding packages, each complete, each under the ceiling, each knowing about the others.

What is joined to what

Every other view in this app places data on a map or on two chosen axes. This one places it by relationship: a force-directed graph in which a node’s position means nothing at all and only its edges carry information. It answers a question the map cannot — which housing stock each announced project actually reaches, and which markets are held together by a single employer or a single campus.

Cancelled projects are drawn crossed out with dashed edges. That is the point of putting them here: the ZIPs still joined to them are ZIPs that were reached by capital which has since been withdrawn, and one picture shows that faster than any table.

Bank-owned and government resale · and what is not obtainable

Completed foreclosures that are now for sale

HUD publishes every FHA single-family home it has taken back after an insured mortgage foreclosed and is now offering for sale — 5,513 of them nationally, with coordinates, carrying no borrower or occupant data of any kind. That is a real bank-owned resale feed from the entity that owns the homes, and it is on this page.

What is not on this page is pre-foreclosure — Notice of Default, Lis Pendens, Trustee’s Sale. That was researched rather than assumed, and it does not exist as free open data. The page says so in the same size as everything else, because the alternative is a tab that looks complete and is not.

Comparable sales desk · recorded transactions only

What somebody actually paid

An underwriting platform lives or dies on comparables, so this desk is strict about what one is. A comparable here is a recorded sale with a date — never an assessor value dressed up as a price, never a sale whose year is unknown, never a parcel in another metro. That strictness costs coverage, and the cost is the point: it is what separates a price from an opinion.

This tab also does something no single record can: it divides what buyers paid by what the assessor published, county by county, and shows you which rolls are behind the market. Two public records, cross-referenced, answering a question neither one answers alone.

Evidence grade · what the public record can and cannot tell you

A row on a map is not evidence

This catalogue pulls from about thirty county sources of wildly unequal quality. One publishes a recorded use, a plain-English zoning string, bedrooms and a real disclosure-state sale price. Another publishes a value and a coordinate and nothing else — no use, no year, no unit count, no sale. Both arrive as a row on the same map. This tab grades every record on what its source actually publishes, so the difference stops being invisible.

A grade is a statement about the paperwork, never about the asset. A grade A record can be a terrible buy and a grade D record can be an excellent one you simply cannot verify yet. What the grade tells you is how much of your diligence the county has already done, and therefore where your own time has to go.

Reconstruction, record locker & 3D

What the record supports — and what it does not

Every property in this edition carries a set of published attributes and a set of silences. This tab separates them: each field is labelled recorded (the jurisdiction publishes it), derived (computed from recorded values) or schematic (a regional convention used only to draw a picture). From that, Locator.X builds a massing model — footprint, floors, roof form, facade — and renders it in 3D on its own and in the context of everything tracked on the block.

These renders are not photographs and not surveys. Colour, material, window rhythm and roof pitch are chosen by rule from region, vintage and use class. A 1920 New Orleans double is drawn as a 1920 New Orleans double because the roll says it is one, not because anyone looked at it. Never show one of these images to a lender, seller or partner as a picture of the building.

The Record Locker underneath does the looking for you. It assembles, per jurisdiction, the assessor record, the deed index, the permit and code file, the flood panel, the street-level panorama and aerial series going back decades — marked direct where the link lands on this address and search where the portal cannot be addressed from a URL. Locator.X opens those doors and never walks through them: nothing here scrapes a portal, and several of these systems state plainly that automated access is abuse. Whatever you find, save into the knowledgebase; it travels as JSON with the deal file.

Pattern mining & predictive automations

What the winners have in common — measured, not asserted

This tab mines every tracked property for the attribute combinations that actually associate with an outcome you care about. Pick an outcome, and the engine computes the base rate across the whole set, then the lift of each attribute and of the strongest attribute pairs — the ratio between the hit rate inside a slice and the hit rate everywhere. Slices below the support floor are discarded rather than reported, because a 100% hit rate on four properties is noise. Turn any pattern into a saved automation and it flags matching inventory on every future pass.

Read these as associations in the data on hand, not causes. Outcomes built on estimated rent or estimated value inherit that estimate's error, and the label on each row says which. Nothing here is a recommendation to buy.

Recommendation for this product. Build on MapLibre GL and keep the map engine free; put money into data instead. Ship two basemap modes: the embedded vector map (this page, works offline and inside any host) and a live tile source — Mapbox Streets if you want 3D buildings and the best cartography (50k loads free covers a launch), or OpenFreeMap/self-hosted PMTiles for zero marginal cost. Stay off Google unless you need Street View, because its terms forbid mixing it with any other map. For interiors, own the assets: 360° photos in a WebGL viewer, with Matterport and Zillow 3D Home links as optional embeds you do not depend on. For listings, sign an IDX/RESO agreement with the local MLS; use RentCast for rent estimates; keep the county assessor rolls as the backbone because they are free, public-domain and, thanks to Prop 13, tell you exactly what every buyer paid.

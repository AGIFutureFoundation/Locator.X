# Mission rights — narrow enough to be financeable, strong enough to be real

A public-benefit corporation whose foundation can veto anything is not governed, it is
paralysed, and no institutional investor will fund it. A PBC whose mission rights are
decorative is not a PBC. The whole design problem is drawing the line in one place and
writing it down before anyone needs it.

**AGI Future Foundation PBC holds limited protective rights, not operating control.**

## Reserved matters — Foundation approval required

| # | Action requiring approval | Why it is on this list |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Amending or removing the defined public-benefit purpose | The purpose is the thing being protected; without this the rest is ornament |
| 2 | Selling or exclusively licensing mission-critical AI safety or governance IP | An exclusive licence is a sale that does not look like one |
| 3 | Dissolving the PBC or materially abandoning its mission commitments | The end-run around every other protection |
| 4 | Removing core AI-safety, accessibility, privacy or human-oversight policies | These are the mission expressed as operating constraints; deleting them deletes the mission quietly |
| 5 | Selling the company in a transaction that materially conflicts with the mission | Addressed in the charter and the exit protocol, not improvised during a deal |
| 6 | Changing the mission-council structure or impact-reporting obligations | Reporting is how anyone outside the room knows any of this is true |
| 7 | Entering a related-party transaction above a defined materiality threshold | The place hidden economics enter a group with this many affiliates |

## Explicitly NOT reserved

The Foundation does **not** approve, and may not block:

- hiring and firing
- pricing decisions
- routine product-roadmap decisions
- enterprise sales contracts
- day-to-day budgets
- financing under a board-preapproved plan
- standard vendor selection

This list matters as much as the one above it. It is the answer to the diligence question
*"can the foundation stop you from running the company?"* — and the answer has to be no,
in writing, with examples.

## The Delaware balancing duty

A Delaware PBC board is required to balance the pecuniary interests of stockholders, the
interests of those materially affected by the corporation's conduct, and the specific
public benefit identified in its charter. A PBC must also provide stockholders, at least
biennially, with a statement covering its objectives, the standards used to measure
progress, factual information about that progress, and an assessment of its success
([Delaware Code title 8, ch. 1, subch. XV](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc15/)).

**This citation is recorded as supplied and has not been verified from this environment**,
which has no egress to external hosts. It is a pointer for counsel, not a legal
conclusion. Nothing here is legal advice, and the charter, the Mission Rights Agreement
and the reserved-matters schedule are counsel-drafted documents that govern over anything
written in this repository.

AGI's intention is to report **annually**, ahead of the biennial statutory cadence, for
the reason that governs the rest of this project: a commitment measured once every two
years is a commitment nobody is managing. Contents are specified in
[`INVESTOR_REPORTING.md`](INVESTOR_REPORTING.md).

## The six documents that make this real

Mission rights asserted in a deck are worth nothing. These are the artefacts that have to
exist, and their absence is a finding rather than an oversight:

| Document | What it settles |
|---|---|
| **Mission Rights Agreement** | The Foundation's narrow reserved matters, exactly as listed above |
| **Conflict-of-Interest Policy** | Covering AGI Corp, the PBC, subsidiaries, founders, advisors and property SPVs |
| **Shared Services Agreement** | IP licences, staff allocation, cost sharing, data rights, transfer pricing |
| **Public Benefit Reporting Policy** | Impact metrics and cadence |
| **Independent Director Policy** | At least one independent director by the first priced round or a defined revenue threshold |
| **Mission-Safe Exit Protocol** | How mission rights are handled in a merger, asset sale or change of control |

**None of the six exists yet.** They are named here so that their absence is a tracked
item rather than a surprise in diligence, and so that a term sheet does not arrive before
the exit protocol does — which is the order in which this goes wrong.

## Why narrow rights protect the mission better than broad ones

Broad mission rights get negotiated away. An investor facing an unbounded veto asks for it
to be removed as a condition of the round, and a founder who needs the round removes it.
A narrow, specific, well-drafted schedule survives that conversation, because it can be
read in an afternoon and it demonstrably does not touch pricing, hiring or roadmap.

The mission is safest when protecting it costs the commercial company almost nothing on
any ordinary day, and everything on the one day that matters.

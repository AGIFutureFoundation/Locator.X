# Who licenses the people in a deal, and how to find them in any state

*Last reviewed: 2026-10. Six occupations touch every transaction this platform teaches —
brokerage, property management, mortgage origination, appraisal, home inspection and
contracting — and each is licensed by a different office under a different statute in
every state. This page does not hold the fifty answers. It holds the **patterns** the
answers follow, the **search recipe** that finds the regulator in any state, the **five
things to read** on any regulator's site, the **federal baselines** that genuinely exist,
and the **verification-record template** the learner fills in — so a licensing fact
reaches a memo only with its source and date attached, the same rule the rest of the
[resource directory](README.md) runs on.*

> **Why there is no table here.** The repository's rule is that no factual row ships
> without a source and a date, and this environment has no internet access: nothing on
> this page has been checked against a regulator's live site, so nothing here is stated
> as a state's rule. There is **no per-state table and no numeric requirement** on this
> page — no hour counts, no fee, no bond amount, no exam score, no renewal period — because
> every one of those is a state fact that changes by statute and that this project
> declines to assert unmeasured. **The follow-up is named:** a sourced fifty-state table,
> one row per state per occupation, added only once each regulator's page has been opened,
> read and dated, with the date in the row. Until then the honest status of every cell is
> *unknown — look it up and record the date*, and the template at the end is how.

> **Verify before relying.** Agencies get renamed and merged, boards move between
> departments, and statutes change with every session. Nothing on this page is legal
> advice or a recommendation; it is navigation of the public record. The regulator's own
> site is the source, and the date you read it is part of the fact.

## 1. The pattern: six occupations, six offices

Licensing is organised by **occupation**, not by deal, so one transaction answers to
several regulators at once. The pattern below holds across most states; the office name
and the statute differ in each.

| Occupation | The usual home of the regulator | What the licence governs |
|------------|---------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Real-estate brokerage** (salesperson, broker) | A real-estate commission or board, often inside a department of commerce, business regulation or licensing | Who may represent a party for compensation; the broker is the licensee responsible for the salespeople under them |
| **Property management** | The same real-estate commission in most states — but see [§4](#4-property-management-three-categories) | Leasing and rent collection for others; whether a licence is needed at all is the state's choice |
| **Mortgage origination** (MLO, mortgage broker/lender) | A banking or financial-institutions department, through the national registry — see [§6](#6-the-federal-baselines-that-exist) | Who may take a residential mortgage application and negotiate terms |
| **Appraisal** | A real-estate appraiser board, sometimes a division of the real-estate commission | Who may sign a valuation that a federally related transaction relies on |
| **Home inspection** | Varies most: a dedicated board, the real-estate commission, the contractor board, or **no regulator at all** | Who may call themselves an inspector; in unlicensed states the answer is anyone, and the protection is the contract |
| **Contracting** | A contractor licensing board, a local registry, or none — see [§5](#5-contractor-licensing-three-categories) | Who may contract for construction above the state's threshold and sign the permit |

Two structural facts follow the pattern everywhere. First, the **firm and the individual
are separate records** — a brokerage and its broker, a mortgage company and its loan
officer, a contracting firm and its qualifying individual — and the lookup that finds one
rarely finds the other. Second, the licence says what the holder **may** do, never that
they did it well; the discipline record ([§3](#3-the-five-things-to-read-on-any-regulators-site-in-order))
is the half of the search most people skip.

## 2. The search recipe that finds the regulator in any state

Search patterns outlive directories. Each line below, with the state's name substituted,
reaches the regulator's licensee lookup in nearly every state on the first page of results;
when it does not, the state's main government portal has an occupational-licensing index
that lists every board.

| Occupation | Search pattern | What a hit looks like |
|------------|----------------|-----------------------|
| Brokerage | `<state> real estate commission licensee lookup` | A state-government domain with a licence search by name, licence number or firm |
| Property management | `<state> property management license required` → then the commission's own FAQ, never a blog | The commission's page saying which activities need a licence |
| Mortgage origination | `NMLS consumer access` (national — one lookup for every state) and `<state> mortgage licensing division` | The national registry's record; the state page for state-specific endorsements |
| Appraisal | `<state> appraiser board license lookup` and the national appraiser registry | The board's search; the national registry confirms federally recognised credentials |
| Home inspection | `<state> home inspector license` — and read the result for the word **no** as carefully as **yes** | Either a board's lookup, or an official page saying the state does not license inspectors |
| Contracting | `<state> contractor license lookup` — if nothing state-wide appears, `<city or county> contractor registration` | A state board search, a local registry, or an official page saying registration is local |

Three rules for the recipe:

1. **Stop at the government domain.** Lead-generation sites, school sites and
   "requirements by state" blogs restate the regulator with a lag and an upsell; the
   regulator's own page is the source and the only one a memo may cite.
2. **Record the date.** The page you read is a fact as of that day. The template in
   [§7](#7-the-verification-record-template) has a column for it because a licensing
   fact without a date is a rumour with a URL.
3. **"No regulator" is a finding.** For home inspection and contracting in particular,
   the honest answer in some states is that no state licence exists. Write *none — no
   state licence; local registration checked at <office>* rather than leaving the cell
   blank; a blank reads as "not yet looked", which is a different state of knowledge
   (the same vocabulary the [coverage inventories](../states/coverage/README.md) use).

## 3. The five things to read on any regulator's site, in order

Every regulator's site, however it is organised, carries the same five documents. Read
them in this order, because each one changes how you read the next.

1. **The licensee lookup.** Is the licence active, what class is it, what firm is it
   attached to, when does it expire. The class matters more than the status: an active
   licence of the wrong class is no licence for the work in front of you.
2. **The discipline and complaint record.** Disciplinary orders, consent agreements,
   revocations, and open complaints where the state publishes them. Many lookups show
   discipline on the licensee's own record; some keep a separate enforcement page. A
   clean lookup with an unread enforcement page is half a check.
3. **The approved education list.** Which schools and courses the regulator accepts for
   pre-licensing and continuing education. For the learner this is the only list that
   counts — a course not on it does not count toward the licence, however good it is.
4. **The exam vendor.** The regulator names the testing company that administers the
   licensing exam and links to its candidate handbook; the handbook, not the regulator,
   holds the content outline, the scheduling rules and the identification requirements.
5. **The rulebook.** The statute and the administrative rules the board enforces, usually
   linked from a "laws and rules" page. This is where the definitions live — what counts
   as brokerage, what counts as managing property, what the contracting threshold is —
   and it is the document that settles every argument the first four start.

The order is the point. The lookup tells you whether a person is licensed; the discipline
record tells you whether to care; the education list and exam vendor tell the learner how
to become one; the rulebook tells everyone what the words on the licence mean.

## 4. Property management: three categories

Whether managing someone else's rental needs a licence is the state's choice, and states
have made three different ones. The commission's own FAQ or rulebook says which; a search
of `<state> property management license required` reaches it, and the answer is one of:

| Category | What it means | How to tell the state uses it |
|----------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| **Broker licence required** | Leasing, showing, negotiating leases or collecting rent for others is defined as real-estate brokerage; a property manager works under a broker's licence, and the firm is a brokerage | The rulebook's definition of brokerage or "real estate activity" lists leasing or rent collection for others; the commission's FAQ says property managers need a licence |
| **Separate PM licence** | The state created a property-management licence or permit distinct from the broker licence, with its own education and exam | The lookup offers a licence type with "property manager" or "property management" in its name |
| **None** | Managing property for others is not licensed activity at the state level; the protections are the contract, trust-account law where it applies, and local rental registration | The commission's FAQ or statute says property management is not brokerage, or the activity is nowhere in the definitions and the commission says so when asked |

Two edges to read for in any category: **on-site managers** of the building they live in
or are employed at are often exempt from a licence the off-site manager needs, and
**short-term rental** management is sometimes treated differently from long-term leasing.
Neither edge is stated here as any state's rule; both are the questions to carry to the
rulebook.

## 5. Contractor licensing: three categories

Contracting divides the same way, and the categories sit on a spectrum rather than in
boxes — a state may license some trades at the state level and leave the rest to cities.
The [trade taxonomy](../../crosswalk/trades.json) that feeds the contractor-matching desk
carries a licence pattern per trade for exactly this reason; the state's own category is
found with `<state> contractor license lookup`:

| Category | What it means | How to tell the state uses it |
|----------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| **State board** | A state contractor licensing board issues licences by classification (general, residential, and the specialty trades), with an exam, a bond and insurance on file, and a lookup that shows all of it | The search returns a state board with a licence search by classification |
| **Local registration** | No state licence for general contracting; cities or counties register contractors, usually with insurance proof and a fee, and the building department enforces it at permit | The state result is an official page saying contractors register locally, or nothing state-wide appears and `<city> contractor registration` does |
| **None** | Neither state nor local registration for the trade in question; the permit is the only gate | The building department's permit page lists no contractor credential among its requirements — confirm by asking the department rather than inferring from silence |

Three refinements hold across categories. **Plumbing, electrical and HVAC are licensed
almost everywhere** even in states with no general-contractor licence, often by a separate
board, so a "none" finding for general contracting says nothing about the trades. The
**classification on the licence**, not the word contractor, decides what the holder may
sign for. And a **right-of-way or health-department permit** (sidewalks, sewer laterals,
septic) answers to an office the contractor board does not run.

## 6. The federal baselines that exist

Three federal floors sit under the state systems. They are the only federal rules this page
names, because they are the only ones this review is confident genuinely exist for these
occupations; a fourth would be invented.

| Occupation | Federal baseline | What it does and does not do |
|------------|------------------|------------------------------|
| **Mortgage origination** | The **SAFE Act** (Secure and Fair Enforcement for Mortgage Licensing Act) and the **Nationwide Multistate Licensing System (NMLS)** it mandates | Requires every residential mortgage loan originator to be either state-licensed or federally registered through NMLS, with a national record anyone can look up. It sets the floor; each state adds its own licensing requirements on top and administers them through the same system |
| **Appraisal** | The **Appraiser Qualifications Board (AQB)** criteria of The Appraisal Foundation, under the federal appraisal framework that created the state boards and the national registry | Sets the minimum education, experience and examination criteria a state must meet to issue a credential that federally related transactions recognise. It is the floor, not the licence: the state board issues the credential and may exceed the criteria |
| **Contracting / renovation** | **EPA Lead Renovation, Repair and Painting (RRP) Rule**, 40 CFR Part 745 | Requires firms performing renovation that disturbs paint in pre-1978 housing and child-occupied facilities to be EPA-certified (or certified by a state EPA has authorized to run the program) and to use a certified renovator and lead-safe practices. It is a federal certification layered on top of whatever contractor licence the state requires — or does not |

There is **no federal licence** for real-estate brokerage, property management or home
inspection; those are state matters entirely, which is why their categories in
[§4](#4-property-management-three-categories) and [§1](#1-the-pattern-six-occupations-six-offices)
run from "licensed" to "none".

## 7. The verification record template

A licensing check is a record or it is a memory. Fill one row per check and keep it with
the deal file; the [closing-packet discipline](../CONTRACT_ANATOMY.md) and the
[decision memo](../states/README.md) both assume it exists.

| Field | What goes in it |
|-------|-----------------|
| **Who** | Firm name and individual name, exactly as the regulator spells them |
| **Occupation / class** | Which of the six, and the licence class or classification the lookup shows |
| **What you checked** | Which of the five documents in [§3](#3-the-five-things-to-read-on-any-regulators-site-in-order) you read — lookup, discipline, education list, exam vendor, rulebook — one line each |
| **Where** | The regulator's name and the URL of the page you read, on a government domain |
| **What it said** | Status, class, expiry, firm attachment, discipline found or none found — in the regulator's words, not a paraphrase |
| **Date** | The day you read it. A check is true as of its date and no later |
| **Category finding** | For property management and contracting, which of the three categories the state uses and the sentence in the rulebook or FAQ that says so |
| **Open questions** | Anything the page did not answer — an exemption that might apply, a class you could not map to the scope — carried to the rulebook or to counsel, never resolved by assumption |

A row with every field filled is a fact the memo may cite. A row with the **Date** or
**Where** field empty is not yet a fact, and the honest thing to write in the memo is
*unverified*.
